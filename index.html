The Irresistible Offer

An offer so good and so specific that the right business owner cannot say no. Before you write a word of yours, here is what makes one.

What an irresistible offer is

An irresistible offer is one so good and so specific that the right business owner cannot say no. Not clever. Not cheap. Specific: this type of business, this problem, this fix, this guarantee. When an owner reads it and thinks "that is exactly my problem and they have clearly fixed it before", most of the sale is done before the call.

Three things make one: why an owner hands over five figures, where their head is at before you speak to them, and whether the market is worth your year.

Why people pay five figures

A loss hurts twice as much as a gain feels good

Ten thousand dollars saved is nice. Ten thousand dollars leaking out every year has to stop. A gain is optional. A loss is not.

You are not creating the loss. It was always there. Everything you build with me exists to make it visible and put a number on it.

Pain always converts to money

Owners describe pain as time, stress, staff, missed calls, slow jobs. Every one of them converts to dollars, and that conversion turns "that's annoying" into "that has to stop".

The pain as they feel itWhat it actually is
"It takes ages."Hours per week, times the hourly cost of whoever does it, times 48 weeks.
"We have someone who handles that."That person's wage, or the share of it spent on work a system could do.
"We miss a few calls."Calls missed per week, times the close rate, times the average job value.
"Jobs take longer than they should."Extra jobs they could complete per month if the bottleneck was gone, times margin.

A construction company pays $13k a year for a CRM and $65k a year for an admin who does what the CRM cannot. Ask the owner and you get "it works fine". Add it up and it is $78k a year to babysit a system. Only the maths changed.

Price lives inside the size of the problem

Once the loss has a number, price stops being about you. A $20k fix for a $2k problem is a disconnect no sales skill fixes. A $50k fix for a $5 million problem is an easy yes. So the question is never "what is my AI worth?" It is "what is this market losing, and what fraction of that is an obvious yes?"

Where their head is at before you call

Take one real estate agent and two offers. "Marketing for real estate agents" has been sold to them for twenty years. They have been pitched it a hundred times, bought it twice and been burned once. They know the problem, the fix, the going rate and every agency's name. You walk in at the top of the ladder, compared to everyone, competing on price.

"An AI that answers and books every enquiry for real estate agents" is a different conversation. They feel the problem every day, nobody has sold them this fix a hundred times, and there is no price in their head yet. You get to name the problem, show it working and set the terms.

The rungs, bottom to top: unaware (no idea there is a problem), problem aware (feel it, can name it), solution aware (know a fix exists, not whose), product aware (comparing options), most aware (ready, want the deal). Build your offer for rungs two and three: name the problem in their words, put a dollar figure on it, show it working. A less aware market is the easier sale.

What a market worth your time looks like

Five tests. All five, and you can check every one from your desk in an afternoon.

  1. They already spend on the problem. A job ad for a receptionist or admin, software they pay for, an answering service, an agency on retainer. If you can name the line on their books that your offer replaces, pass. You are redirecting money that is already leaving.
  2. The pain has a dollar figure. You can write the yearly cost in one line: calls missed a week, times close rate, times job value. Hours a week, times wage, times 48. If the best you can get to is "it's frustrating", fail.
  3. They have the money. Three or more staff, premises or vehicles, work booked ahead. Google them: a team page, more than one van, more than one location. A solo operator is a fail for a five figure system, no matter how nice they are.
  4. The decision maker is reachable. The owner's name on the website and a mobile you can find in ten minutes: pass. Trades, clinics and agencies answer their phones. "Send it to info@", a head office, a procurement form: fail.
  5. There is one problem they would pay to have solved tomorrow. Not "they could use AI". One thing that is broken right now: enquiries going unanswered, quotes going out late, leads going cold, a person spending their week on admin. Find the problem they need solved and the offer writes itself.

Let Jarvis score it

Do not score markets by hand. Give Jarvis the niche and let it run the five tests with real research behind each one.

Score this market for me: [niche, size of business, country or city]. Run the five tests. For each one give me a score from 1 to 5, the evidence you found, and the one line that proves it: 1. They already spend on this problem (what they pay for today). 2. The pain has a dollar figure (write the yearly cost as a formula). 3. They have the money (staff, premises, revenue signals). 4. The decision maker is reachable (where their name and number show up). 5. The one problem they would pay to have solved tomorrow (name it in their words). Add the scores. 20 or more means go. 15 to 19 means narrow the niche and tell me how. Under 15 means move on and suggest two markets next door that would score higher.

Residential builders behind my $22k CRM deal score 24. Small cafes score 12. Lovely people, not a market for five figure systems. And pick one market, not five. "We build systems for residential builders" earns trust before you say anything else, and your demo, proof and outreach all compound in one niche.

The process

Find the niche that passes the five tests. Find the one problem they would pay to have solved tomorrow. Write the offer from that.

Most people get stuck here, not on the selling. They sit with a blank page trying to invent an offer from nothing. You do not have to invent anything. Here are ten offers that are selling right now, with the businesses running them. For each one: what it actually is, how it works, who buys it and why, and the offer written out in six lines.

Read them properly. You cannot sell something you half understand. I could sell anyone a glass of water, because I know exactly what water does and I believe in it. That certainty is calm, and calm is what closes. It comes from knowing the offer cold, not from a script.

How this works

Somebody has already spent months and real money proving that this type of business pays for this result. You do not copy them. You move the proven offer one step.

  • Same offer, new niche. A receptionist that sells to dentists sells to physios, vets and groomers.
  • Same offer, new country. Most of these are proven in the US and barely run anywhere else.
  • Same niche, slightly different offer. They sell the leads, you sell the follow up that stops those leads going cold.

Nobody keeps paying for 90 versions of an offer that loses money. And where an offer needs leads, the leads come from simple ads. A website tells people who you are. A landing page takes one problem, says it out loud, and puts the right person on your calendar.

A website is for brand awareness. Who you are, what you do, the clients you have worked with, their logos, a bit of story. It makes you look credible. It does not sell.

A landing page does one job. One specific problem you solve, said at its core, what it costs them, how you fix it, and one button: book the call. A plumber does not want "AI automation". They want the phone answered when every hand is on a job. The landing page says that.

Have both if you want. But every ad you run and every message you send points at the landing page, never the website. The website is where people go afterwards to check you are real.

The copy is not new work. It is your six lines, said to one person with one problem. If the page is hard to write, go back to the offer.

Module 5 · Client Acquisition: Paid Ads · Lesson 1 of 2

How Ads Actually Work

Ads are the most consistent way I know to scale this business. They come down to what you say on camera and four numbers.

Ads are one of the best things you can crack in this business. Outreach gets you your first clients and teaches you the market. Ads are what scale it. I want you to truly understand how they work for an offer like yours, which numbers decide whether you are winning, and what to do with every ad you run.

Why ads are so powerful

Outreach is one to one. You call an owner, you have the conversation, you move to the next one. Ads are one to many. You say the message once, on camera, and Meta puts it in front of every business owner it can find who looks like your buyer. In every city you choose. While you sleep.

Ads are not cheap or easy at the start. They are the most consistent way to scale, because when a message works you turn the budget up without finding more hours in your week.

The whole game in one line

Test as many angles as possible and find as many winners as possible. An angle is one way of talking about your offer: the problem costing the owner money, the result they want, the objection already in their head. Put each one in front of real people, the market tells you which ones land, and you make more of those.

The words do the targeting now

Ads used to be about the settings: ages, interests, job titles, cities. Meta's new ad system changed that. What you say in your video is what finds the right person. Meta watches who stops scrolling, who keeps watching, who clicks, and goes and finds more people like them.

Open with "if you run a plumbing business with more than five vans and your office is drowning in quotes" and Meta finds plumbers with five vans and a drowning office. Open with "AI is changing everything" and Meta finds people who like AI videos, and none of them buy a five figure system. The thing that decides your results is the thing you control: the words, and who they are aimed at.

Be specific or it does not work

The more precisely your ad names the owner and the pain, the cheaper every number in your account gets. Your niche, their problem, their words: that is your ad, said out loud to a stranger who gives you eight seconds.

What a customer can cost you

If you can acquire a customer for less than you get paid, it works. That is the floor. The number is CAC, cost to acquire a customer: all the ad spend it took, divided by the number of clients who paid.

At the very least I want you at a 50 percent CAC. Sell for $5,000, spend $2,500 to get that client. That is a pretty bad CAC, but it still works and it does not break you while you learn. Never run a number you cannot afford to lose while an ad is still being tested.

It also depends on your ticket. At $35,000 you can afford a far higher cost per click, lead and booked call than at $1,000, because one close pays for a lot of misses. The trade is a smaller pool of people who can pay it.

Your four numbers

Work backwards from your price, before you spend a dollar. Say you sell at $8,000 and you want CAC under 25 percent. That gives you $2,000 per client won. Close one in four qualified calls and each booked call can cost up to $500. One in three leads books a call, so each lead can cost up to about $165. One winner, many versions

Once you find a winning angle, do not go hunting for a new idea. Make versions of it: different opening line, different setting, same words as a static image. Every ad wears out and a fresh version resets the cost. My rhythm: test three to five angles, find one winner, make five versions, run those while you test the next batch.

Scale, fix or kill

Give each ad the cost of one or two booked calls before you judge it, and never on its first day. Then it goes one of three ways.

  1. Scale. Booking qualified calls at or under target. Raise the budget around 20 percent every two or three days. Make versions. Do not touch the ad itself.
  2. Fix. One number is broken, the rest are fine. Clicks but expensive leads: the page does not match the ad. Leads but no bookings: your questions or booking flow. Bookings but no shows: the follow up. Fix that one step. Do not rewrite the ad.
  3. Kill. Fair spend, cost per qualified call well over target, or nobody clicking. Turn it off and write down why in one line. A dead ad with a reason is data. A dead ad you keep running out of hope is a bill.

Log every decision in your CRM: ad, outcome, reason, next action. In a month that record tells you exactly what this market responds to.

One Conversation, Two Calls

I am not selling on the first call. I am diagnosing. The sale happens when I come back with the fix, and I want you selling exactly the same way.

This is how I sell. One conversation, usually split across two calls, built so that by the time the price comes out, saying no feels like choosing to keep the problem. It is behind every deal I have closed over five figures and you can run it on your second day.

A bit of psychology before anything else. Most people open with "thanks so much for jumping on today". Do not. They booked into your calendar because something you said hit a pain. You are the one they came to for the fix. Thanking them for their time puts you below them before you have asked a single question. You are the prize in that room. Act like it: calm, curious, warm. Never grateful.

The call structure that has closed $22k+ deals

Call one is diagnosis. I find the pain, I put a dollar figure on it, and I get them to agree to that figure out loud. No pitch, no demo, no price. Call two is the prescription. I present what I would build, tied to what they told me, show them a demo, anchor the price against the number they already agreed to, say my number, and stop talking. In between, Jarvis and I build the proposal.

The doctor frame

Think about how a specialist runs an appointment. You booked in because something hurts. They do not thank you for turning up and they do not quote a surgery fee before they have examined you. They ask what is wrong, how long, what you have tried. Then they come back with a plan.

That is the frame. They came to you for the fix. Diagnose, then prescribe. If you believe you are lucky to be on the call, every question sounds like permission seeking and every number sounds like an apology. If you believe you are the doctor, the same words land completely differently.

One call or two?

Your offer decides this, so decide it before your first call.

If your offer is specific, a receptionist, a follow up system, a pay per lead campaign, you do not need two calls. You already know what you are going to build because it is the same thing every time. Run the diagnosis, get the number, then say "give me a sec, I'll grab your proposal and we can go through it". Jarvis takes your template proposal and brands it to them while you are still talking. Walk them through it, anchor, say the number, close. One call.

If your offer is custom, a CRM, a bespoke system, anything you have to design for that business, you need two calls. Not as a tactic. Because you genuinely need to go back and plan the whole build with your technical partner before you can tell them what it costs. That is also the reason you give them for booking call two, and it is true, so it lands.

New? Start with a specific offer. Shorter calls, a template proposal, fast reps. Graduate to custom.

Why no price on call one

Your price is a fraction of what the problem costs them. Until they have told you what it costs, any number is a guess that gets compared to other guesses. When they ask on call one, and some will:

I could throw a number at you now, but it would be a guess, and you deserve better than a guess. Let me go away, map exactly what this would look like for you, get the real cost of building it, and come back with a proper proposal. If the numbers make sense we move. Before you say a word

You are the prize. They booked into your calendar because something you said hit a pain and they want it fixed. So you do not open with "thank you for jumping on today". That line makes them the chooser and you the applicant. Warm, curious, unhurried. Never grateful. And remember the rule that runs the whole call: the one asking the questions is in control. You ask. Step 4. The four questions that find the number

This is the core of the call. Everything before it was warming up. Four questions, in this order, turn a complaint into a dollar figure they will agree to.

  1. What is actually happening? The problem as events.
  2. What are you doing about it right now? Tools, people, workarounds, nothing.

Prescribe the Solution

They gave you a problem and a number. Now you design the thing that fixes it, check it can be built, and price it against the problem, not the work.

Two Ways To Deliver

You have money in the bank and a promise to keep. The first decision is who builds it.

The deposit has landed. You owe a business owner a working system, and how you deliver it decides whether this deal makes you money or eats the next three weeks of your life. There are two ways to deliver. Pick one before you touch a single build.

The two paths

Arbitrage. You sold it, a developer builds it. You scope the job, hand them a build plan, pay a fixed price in milestones, and keep the difference between what the client paid you and what the developer cost. Your job is the client, the scope and the money. Their job is the build.

Self fulfilment. You sold it and you build it, with Jarvis doing most of the work. Same build plan, handed to Jarvis one piece at a time, tested, delivered by you. Higher margin, more of your hours, and it only works if you are comfortable in the tools.

My honest recommendation

If you are not technical, bring someone technical in to finish the job. Every time. Do the creative side yourself: the workflow design, the client conversations, the demo. Then hand the build to someone who already knows how to wire it, instead of learning it from scratch while a paying client waits.

That is not a comment on your ability. You just proved you can find a business owner, diagnose a real problem and get paid five figures to fix it. That skill is rare and it is the one that scales. Debugging an integration at eleven at night is not rare, and plenty of good people will do it for a fixed fee. The people who stall here are never the ones who could not sell. They sold, then disappeared into a build they were not ready for.

Arbitrage: Find and Vet Developers

Your job is the client and the money. Their job is the build. Getting this bench right is the whole model.

Handing a build off only works if you have someone to hand it to before you need them. The worst time to find a developer is the week after a deposit lands. Build a bench of two or three people you have already tested, so when a deal closes the build plan goes out the same day.

Start with my list

I keep a list of developers we have vetted on the kinds of systems you are selling. Ask for it in the group or at the weekly strategy session. Still find one of your own, so you are never waiting on someone else's calendar.

Where else to look

  • Upwork. The biggest pool, easy to post to, escrow protects both sides. Most people find their first builder here.
  • Your own network. Anyone who has had an automation or an app built for a real business. A referral from someone who paid for the work beats fifty profiles.
  • The communities around the tools. People who answer questions for free in the forums for the client's platform usually build for money too.

Jarvis writes the job post

A good job post describes the client's business, the specific thing being built and the skills it needs, in that order. "AI automation expert needed" gets two hundred replies from people who have never shipped anything.

Write an Upwork job post for a developer to build this system. Here is my offer [paste] and the build plan for this client [paste the plan from the scoping skill, or the proposal if the plan is not done yet]. Structure it as: one paragraph on the client's business and the problem in plain words; a short list of exactly what is being built, including every tool it has to connect to; the specific skills and tools the developer must have already used, named; what I will provide (the full build plan, test data, access); how I want to work (fixed price, three milestones, a short paid test task first, daily written updates); and two questions they must answer in their proposal that prove they read the post. Under 300 words. Do not mention the client's name. Do not use the word expert.

Posting on Upwork

  1. Create a client account under your business name and verify a payment method.
  2. Post the job. Fixed price, not hourly. Budget at the developer cost you priced the deal on, not what the client paid.
  3. Set the two screening questions. Anyone who does not answer them gets deleted unread.
  4. Invite three to five profiles whose completed jobs match the tool stack. Ignore hourly rate and skills lists.
  5. Wait 48 hours. Shortlist three. Reply to nobody else.

How I vet them

Three things predict whether a developer will deliver. None of them is their profile.

A small paid test task. Pay each shortlisted person for one small piece of the actual job you can check in an hour: connect to the test CRM and create a record, parse one sample email into fields. You are watching whether they ask or guess, and whether what comes back works.

Past work you can see. One thing a real business uses today, and a two minute recording of it working. Not a portfolio page.

How they reply. Inside a working day, short plain sentences, a question when unclear, a screenshot when done. Slow or vague on a paid test will be worse on the real build.

Rates and the shape of the deal

Fixed price in three milestones: built on test data, tested and reviewed by you, live and handed over. A third each, released when you have seen it work. Never everything up front. Get three quotes against the same plan and you know the market inside two days. My rule: the developer cost sits well under half of what the client paid for the build. If it does not, the deal was priced wrong, not the developer.

Keep the developer and the client apart. You are the account. Updates go through you, the developer never sees the client's price, the client never sees the developer's quote. That is what keeps the margin.

Arbitrage: Scope, Hand Off, Keep the Margin

A bad handover is how a profitable deal turns into three weeks of unpaid project management.

You know more about this client's business than anyone about to build for them, and all of it lives in notes, a proposal and your head. Turn it into one document a developer can build from without a single clarifying call. That document protects your margin.

Why the scope is the whole job

Every expensive build problem is a scoping problem in disguise. The brief said automate intake and never said what happens when the email has no phone number. Nobody wrote down which of their three spreadsheets was the real one. All of it was knowable the day the deal closed. Writing it down then costs an hour. Finding it out later costs the margin.

The scoping skill

The scoping skill is in your Jarvis pack. Feed it your call one notes and the number you agreed, your call two notes, the proposal as presented, and anything the client sent you. It gives back one document, always in this shape.

Read the unknowns list first. Most are one message to the client: which spreadsheet is current, who approves a big quote, what their booking tool is called. Send it today. Then read the edge cases and decide any the client never mentioned, or the developer decides them for you. Never send a plan with unknowns open. A plan with holes gets a quote with holes, and the holes become change requests at your cost.

The handoff

  1. Send the plan, ask for a fixed quote against the three milestones. Two working days.
  2. Agree price and dates in writing. Per milestone, released on acceptance. A deposit for milestone one is normal, all of it up front is not.
  3. Give them test data and sandbox access, never the client's live accounts.
  4. Set the rhythm. A short written update every working day, a screen recording at every milestone, questions come to you.
  5. Log it against the deal in your CRM: developer, quote, milestone dates, what has been paid.

You are the account manager, not the messenger. When the developer asks something you do not know, ask the client as if it is your question, because it is. The client bought you.

Keeping the margin

The margin is lost in three places: scope that grew without a price change, milestones paid before they were checked, and your hours disappearing into chasing. The plan fixes the first. Acceptance tests fix the second: a milestone is paid when the tests pass on a recording you watched. The daily update fixes the third. When the client asks for something not in the plan: "Good idea. It is not in this build, so I will scope it as a follow on once we are live." Scope creep is the next invoice, as long as it never gets built for free.

Made up example. You sell a quoting system to a landscaper. The proposal says quotes come from a site visit form. Your call two notes show half the enquiries come in by text. The scoping skill puts "text message intake: in scope or follow on?" at the top of the unknowns. You ask, it matters, and it becomes a $1,800 follow on instead of an unpaid surprise in week three.

Run the skill, clear every unknown with the client today, send the plan to the two developers on your bench for a fixed quote.

Self Fulfilment: Build It With Jarvis

The same Jarvis that helped you sell it can build most of it. Twenty small jobs, in order, each checked before the next.

The build is not one task. It is twenty small ones, done in order, each checked before the next. Jarvis does most of the typing. You do the ordering, the checking and the judgement. The people who struggle here are not short on skill. They asked Jarvis to build the whole system in one message and spent a week untangling what came back.

Same plan, different builder

You still start with the scoping skill and clear the unknowns with the client first. Skip the developer budget, but give yourself dates. A build with no go live date drifts. Tempted to add something? Is it in the plan? If not, it waits.

Build the way Jarvis builds best

  1. Set up the shell first. The empty app, the empty tables, the connections to the tools with test credentials. Confirm each connection reads and writes one record before moving on.
  2. Build modules in plan order, which is the order a job moves through the system. Intake before processing, processing before output. Nobody has ever been paid for a dashboard that shows nothing.
  3. One module per message. Paste that module's section of the plan and ask for only that. Run its acceptance test. Pass, move on. Fail, fix that one thing before touching anything else.
  4. Edge cases get their own messages. Happy path first, tested, then each edge case from the plan as its own change, tested on its own.
  5. Human steps get built as real steps. A status a person changes, a message a person receives. Do not let Jarvis quietly automate a decision the plan says a person makes.

The build message I use every time

We are building module [number], [name], from this build plan [paste the plan once at the start of the session, then refer to it]. Build only this module. Here is its section of the plan: [paste]. Use the test data in [where it is]. Do not touch any other module. When it is done, tell me exactly what you built, what you did not build, and how I run the acceptance test for this module from the plan. If anything in the plan is unclear or impossible in this tool, stop and ask me before building.

Going back and forth

Jarvis builds a piece, you test it with a real example, you say what is wrong in plain words, it fixes it. "It doesn't work" produces guesses. "I sent the sample email with no phone number and it created a job with a blank phone field instead of flagging it like the plan says" produces a fix.

  • Describe the input, what happened, and what should have happened. Every time, in that order.
  • One fix per message. Three things wrong, three messages. Batching them is how the second fix undoes the first.
  • Ask it to explain before it changes anything big. "Before you change this, tell me in two sentences what you think is causing it." Half the time you spot the real cause yourself.

Keep the client's live data out of the build. Every record you test on is made up or a sample with real names removed. The first time real data touches the system is the go live at handover, with the client watching.

When to stop and get help

One integration has eaten more than a couple of hours, the tool cannot do something the plan assumes, or you have fixed the same bug three times: stop. Bring it to the weekly strategy session, or hand that one module to a developer from your bench at a fixed price while you keep building the rest. Most people who have done a few deals do both inside the same one.

Made up example. You build an enquiry triage system for a plumber. Intake and the job record take two evenings with Jarvis. The booking tool needs an API you have never used. You pay a developer $400 fixed to wire that one connection against the plan, and keep building while you wait.

Track it like a project

Keep a build log in Jarvis against the deal. It becomes the record of what was delivered.

Create a build log for the [client] deal from the build plan. One row per module with: built, acceptance test passed, edge cases done, and notes. Update it every time I tell you a module is done or a test has passed. Each time I ask, show me the log and tell me the next module to build in plan order and any client inputs still outstanding.

Set up the shell, build module one, run its test, tick the log. The rest is the same loop.

Self Fulfilment: Test It Like the Client Will

Clients do not use systems the way you built them. Find that out yourself, in private.

Every module passed its own test. That is not the same as the system working. The client is going to put a real job through it, start to finish, on a busy Tuesday, from their phone, with a typo in the email address. Before they see it, you run it the way they will, end to end, and fix what breaks in private.

Why I never skip this

The first time a client sees their system is the moment they decide whether you are a professional. If the first job they push through gets stuck, the build could be ninety five percent perfect and they will remember the five. If it runs clean, they forgive small things for months. Testing is about controlling the first impression of something you have been paid five figures for.

It is also where the retainer gets sold without saying a word. A client who watches a system handle a messy edge case properly understands why it is worth paying to keep it running.

Three jobs, end to end

  1. The clean job. Everything present, everything normal. It goes all the way through with no human stepping in except where the plan says they do.
  2. The messy job. A missing field, a wrong format, a duplicate of a job already in the system. Each lands where the plan says, flagged for a person, not silently dropped and not silently guessed.
  3. The job from the client's seat. On a phone, logged in as the role that will actually use it, not as you with full access. Half of what you find will be about what that person can and cannot see.

Go back and forth with Jarvis the same way you built it. Input, what happened, what should have happened. One fix per message. Rerun the job from the start after every fix, because a fix in step four can break step two.

Jarvis writes the checklist from the plan

From this build plan [paste] and this build log [paste], write me an end to end test checklist. Three test jobs: one clean, one with the edge cases from the plan's edge case section, and one run as the [client role] user on a phone. For each job list every step from first input to final output, what I should see at each step, and what counts as a fail. Add the human steps from the plan as explicit checks that a person is actually asked. Format it as a checklist I can tick, with a column for the result and a column for notes. Plain language, no technical terms the client would not understand, because I am going to show them this.

Keep the log

Write down what you ran, what failed, what you fixed, and that it passed on the rerun. Date it. It proves to you the system is ready, it becomes the first page of the handover so the client sees the edge cases were thought about, and it is the raw material for your case study.

If a developer built it, this still applies to you. Do not accept the final milestone on their word. Run the three jobs yourself, on the recording or the test system, and release payment when they pass.

Then show the client, a little

Once the three jobs pass clean, send the client a short screen recording of the clean job going through, with one line: "Here is a job going through your system start to finish. Handover is booked for [date] and I will walk you through it then." No login yet. No poking at it unsupervised. They get their hands on it at handover, with you beside them.

Three passes from the start, dated, in the log. Then book the handover.

Onboard, Hand Over, Get Paid

As far as the client is concerned, the experience is the product. This is also where referrals get made or lost.

The system works. Now it has to work for them, run by their people, with their real data. Handover is a process, not a login sent by email. Done properly it is one call and a week of light contact. Done badly it is a month of "quick questions" and a client who never quite trusts the thing they paid for.

Before you book the call

  • The test log is clean. Three end to end passes, dated. If a developer built it, you ran them yourself.
  • The client has done their part. Access to the live tools, the real records to load, the names of the people who will use it. The unknowns list is empty.
  • You know who is running the technical side. If you are not technical, the person who built it or someone from your bench is on the call flicking the switches while you run the conversation. You own the relationship. They own the switches.

Nobody should do their first handover alone. Bring it to the weekly strategy session, or ask me for someone from the developer list.

The handover call

An hour, on a screen share, with the owner and whoever uses it day to day.

  1. Remind them what they bought and why. Two minutes. The problem in their words, the number it was costing them, what this system does about it.
  2. Walk the clean job through, live. Say what is happening at each step and who gets told. Then the messy job, so they see an edge case flagged to a person instead of disappearing. This is the single most trust building thing you will do.
  3. Go live, with them watching. Load the first real records. Pick one real job that is in their business right now and push it through. Fix nothing on the fly. If something is wrong, note it and say when it will be fixed.
  4. Train the people who will use it. Each role does their part once, themselves, while you watch. A person who has clicked the button will click it again. A person who watched you click it will message you on Monday.
  5. Sign off and the support window. Go through the acceptance tests on screen, tick them together, and ask the owner to confirm in writing that the build is delivered as scoped.

That is everything we scoped, built and tested. You have just watched a real job go through it and your team has run their parts. I am going to send you this checklist with what we ticked, and I need one line back from you confirming it is delivered as agreed. From today you have thirty days of support included: anything that does not behave the way we showed you, send it to me and it gets fixed, no charge. On day thirty we have a short call about keeping it running.

Sign off, then invoice

The final invoice goes out the day you get the written sign off, not before. Send it with the ticked checklist and the test log attached, so the invoice is a record of what was delivered rather than a request for money. Clients who can see what they got pay faster. If a developer built it, release their final milestone the same day, against the same checklist.

Support, Retainer and Raising the Price

The install was the start. Everything after it is margin, proof and a higher price next time.

You have been paid for the build. Three things in the next thirty days decide whether that was one deal or the start of a business: support it properly, turn it into a monthly retainer, and turn the result into the reason the next client pays more. None of them is hard. All of them get skipped by people already chasing the next sale.

The thirty day support window

Thirty days from sign off, and defined. Included: anything that does not behave the way it was shown at handover, questions from the people using it, small wording changes. Not included: new modules, new integrations, anything on the follow on list. Those are change requests with a price, said warmly the moment one comes up. Reply inside a working day, fix inside two for anything that stops a job moving, and keep every request in the deal record.

What the retainer is

Systems do not run themselves. Tools update, a login expires, a new hire needs training. The retainer is the monthly fee that keeps the system working and keeps you as the person they call. It is usually the most profitable money in the deal, because the work is light and it adds up across clients.

Write the case study now

The test log, the build log and the first monthly report are a case study that has not been written yet. Write it in the first month while the numbers are fresh and the client is pleased, and get their written permission before anything goes on a page, an ad or a call.

Do not publish anything; I will get the client's written permission first.

Raise the price

Your next price is set by your last result. Before this deal you were selling a promise. After it you are selling a thing that exists, with a number next to it. The build is still priced against what the problem costs, but your range moves up, and the proposal now opens with proof.

  • Put the two sentence case study into call one, right after you have found the pain: "we did this for a [similar business] last month."
  • Add the retainer to every proposal from now on as a standard line.
  • Decide the next price today, write it in your offer document, and tell the group session so someone holds you to it.

Made up numbers. First quoting system, $9,000, no retainer. Day thirty you offer $600 a month and they take it. Your next proposal opens with quotes going out the same day instead of in three, prices the build at $14,000, and includes the retainer from the start. Same work. One proof point.

Offer the retainer, draft the case study, write the new price down and tell someone.
