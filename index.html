The Irresistible Offer

An offer so good and so specific that the right business owner cannot say no. This lesson is what makes one, before you write a word of yours.

They leave with
They leave understanding what an irresistible offer is and the three ideas underneath every one: why owners pay five figures, where the buyer's head is at, and what makes a market worth it.

What an irresistible offer is

An irresistible offer is one so good and so specific that the right business owner cannot say no. Not clever. Not cheap. Specific: this type of business, this loss, this fix, this result, this guarantee. When an owner reads it and thinks "that is exactly my problem and they have clearly fixed it before", most of the sale is done before the call.

You cannot write one until you understand three things: why an owner hands over five figures, where their head is at before you ever speak to them, and what makes one market worth your year and another a waste of it. That is this lesson. It is education, so read it and watch Blake walk through it. The next lesson turns it into the process: find the niche, find the service they want, write the offer in six lines.

Why people pay five figures

THE FEAR OF LOSING BEATS THE JOY OF GAINING

In 1979 Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published prospect theory, the work that later won Kahneman a Nobel Prize. The finding that matters to you is simple. People do not weigh gains and losses evenly. A loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equal gain feels good. Ten thousand dollars saved is nice. Ten thousand dollars leaking out every year is a problem that has to stop.

This is why "this could save you time" gets a polite nod and nothing else. A gain is optional. A loss is not. The owner who realises they are paying a person $65k a year to do work a system could do, and have been for three years, cannot un-know that. The loss sits in front of them every day until it is fixed.

You are not creating the loss. It was always there. Everything you build in this course, the offer, the outreach, the demo, the call, exists to make a real loss visible and put a number on it.

PAIN ALWAYS CONVERTS TO MONEY

Owners describe pain in all sorts of currencies. Time. Stress. Staff. Missed calls. Slow jobs. None of those are the unit anyone buys in. Every one of them converts to dollars, and the conversion is what turns "that's annoying" into "that has to stop".

The pain as they feel itWhat it actually is
"It takes ages."Hours per week, times the hourly cost of whoever does it, times 48 weeks.
"We have someone who handles that."That person's wage, or the share of it spent on work a system could do.
"The software does most of it."The subscription, plus per job or per user fees, plus the support tickets nobody counts.
"We miss a few calls."Calls missed per week, times the close rate, times the average job value.
"Jobs take longer than they should."Extra jobs they could complete per month if the bottleneck was gone, times margin.

An illustrative example. A mid sized construction company runs its jobs through a generic CRM at around $13k a year. An office administrator works three days a week on tasks the CRM cannot do: chasing sign offs, re-entering quotes, sending reminders. She costs over $65k a year. In busy months they turn work away. Ask the owner how things are going and you get "we've got a CRM and an admin, it works fine". Add it up and the same business is paying roughly $78k a year for a system that still needs a human to babysit it. Nothing changed between those two sentences. Only the maths did. Numbers of the kind Blake has seen in real deals, here to show the conversion, not as a client result.

PRICE LIVES INSIDE THE SIZE OF THE PROBLEM

Once the loss has a number, price stops being about you. If a problem costs a business $2k a year and you try to charge $20k to solve it, there is a disconnect no sales skill fixes. Flip it. If the problem costs them $5 million a year, $50k for a website is a sensible decision, assuming the solution genuinely solves it. To them it is not a cost. It is an investment that pays back many times over.

The Australian government spent $96 million on a website. Bloated, yes, extreme, yes. It happened because the budget existed and the problem was worth that much to them. Price always lives inside the size of the problem.

So the question is never "what is my AI worth?" The question is "what is this market losing, and what fraction of that is an obvious yes?" A small slice of a real, quantified problem is an easy yes. A big slice of a vague one is impossible. How that becomes an actual price on an actual call is Module 6. For now, hold the principle.

The five stages of awareness

The most useful idea in marketing is sixty years old. Eugene Schwartz wrote Breakthrough Advertising in 1966, and the ideas in it still run most successful campaigns on the internet. His central idea: a buyer's state of awareness decides what your message has to say first. Not your product. Where their head is at. Five stages. Each one up, the buyer knows more and needs less from you.

1. UNAWARE

They do not know they have a problem. The owner whose admin "handles all that". Lead with AI, or even with the problem, and you bounce straight off. The only thing that reaches them is a symptom they recognise: "Ever had a quote sit in the inbox for three weeks and nobody noticed?"

2. PROBLEM AWARE

They feel the pain and can name it, but they do not know a fix exists, or they assume the fix is "hire someone" or "work harder". "We keep missing calls after five." "The office is flat out." Lead with the problem, in their words, and the cost of it. Make the loss visible before any solution appears.

3. SOLUTION AWARE

They know the kind of fix exists. "AI could probably do this." "There must be a system for that." They just do not know whose, or whether it would work for them. Lead with the solution, shown, not described: a demo, a before and after, a worked case.

4. PRODUCT AWARE

They know you, or they know the options. They are comparing. Lead with what makes your version the right one for them specifically: the proof, the fit, the delivery, the running costs laid out honestly. Do not explain the problem again. It reads as padding.

5. MOST AWARE

They want it. They just need the deal. Price, terms, start date, what happens next. Lead with the offer. Anything else is in the way.

THE TWO MISTAKES THAT COST THE MOST

Pitching the product to the unaware. "We build AI receptionists for trades" in front of an owner who does not think missed calls are a problem is not wrong, it is early.

Explaining the problem to the most aware. The owner who said "send me the proposal" and gets three paragraphs on why missed calls cost money feels talked down to.

Both come from the same habit: saying what you want to say instead of what they are ready to hear.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOUR OFFER

A market is not one buyer, it is a spread. Where the weight of the market sits changes what your offer has to say and how much educating you will do before anyone buys.

What a desired market is

People pick a market because they know someone in it, then spend a quarter discovering nobody there has money or pain. A desired market is one where the maths already works before you arrive. It passes five tests. All five.

1. THEY ALREADY SPEND ON THE PROBLEM

The best signal that a market will pay you is that it is already paying someone. A part time admin. A $13k a year platform. A call answering service. Existing spend proves the problem is real enough to pay for and the money is already in the budget. You are not asking for new money. You are redirecting money that is already leaving.

2. THE PAIN HAS A DOLLAR FIGURE

A desired market is one where you can get to the dollars in one conversation. Missed calls times job value. Jobs turned away times margin. Wages on removable work. If the pain only ever converts to "it's frustrating", the market is not desired, however much they complain.

3. THEY HAVE THE MONEY

Pain without budget is charity work. If they employ people and own equipment or premises, a five figure system that removes a wage is a normal decision for them. If they are a solo operator doing $90k a year, it is not, no matter how much they like you.

4. THE DECISION MAKER IS REACHABLE

Trades, construction, clinics, agencies, distributors: owners answer their phones and their DMs. Enterprise and government: the person you reach is never the person who decides. Reachability is the difference between a thirty day sales cycle and a nine month one.

5. A HUMAN IS DOING WORK A SYSTEM COULD DO

Someone in the business is manually chasing, re-entering, reminding, answering, scheduling or reporting. That person's hours are the thing you remove, and that is where your price comes from. If the work is already automated well, you are not needed. If it is genuinely human judgement, you cannot replace it. The sweet spot is repeatable work done by a person because nobody built the system yet.

WHY ONE MARKET BEATS FIVE

"We build systems for residential builders" earns trust before you say anything else. "We do AI for businesses" has to be convinced. Specialists charge more and sell faster for the same work. Your demo compounds: build one for a builder and it works, with light changes, for the next fifty. Your proof compounds: one case study in a niche beats ten across random industries, because the prospect sees themselves in it. Your outreach gets simpler: one list, one message, one set of objections. "But I'm limiting myself" is the feeling. You are not. There are tens of thousands of businesses in any decent niche. You are limiting how many problems you have to understand at once, which is the thing that keeps beginners stuck.

The process

Everything above is the thinking. Creating the irresistible offer is a process, and it runs in one direction.

  1. Find the underserved niche. A market that passes the five tests, where the same loss repeats business after business, big enough to be worth five figures to stop. Lesson: Find the Underserved Markets.
  2. Find the service they desire. The specific thing that market is already paying a human or a bad tool to do, that a system could do instead. Lesson: Find the Custom Implementations.
  3. Create the offer from that. One page. Who it is for, the loss it removes, what gets installed, what it costs them to keep the problem. Lesson: Write the Offer.

The strategy session and the technical scope sit between steps two and three, so you never write an offer for something that cannot be built.

Three Ways to Find an Offer That Scales

You do not invent an offer. You find one that is already proven, or a problem nobody is solving, and write it down in six lines.

They leave with
One offer written in the six line formula, found through one of three proven routes.

Most people in this programme get stuck here, not on the selling. They sit with a blank page trying to invent an offer from nothing. You do not have to invent anything. There are three ways to find an offer that is already proven to scale, and your Jarvis does most of the digging on all three. Pick one door, walk through it, and come out the other side with one offer line you can build the whole business on.

Each way has its own skill in your Jarvis skill pack. Way one is jarvis-ad-library-offer-research. Way two is jarvis-offer-menu, which reads the offers list below. Way three is jarvis-custom-opportunity-finder. Run one, not all three.

Way 1: study what is already scaling

Every ad on Meta is public. The Meta Ad Library lets anyone see every ad a page is running right now, how long it has run, and what it says. That is a free window into which offers are working, because nobody keeps spending on an offer that does not convert.

The signal you are looking for is simple. One offer, lots of creatives, running for a long time. If a page has 15 or more ads live for the same offer, they are scaling it. Testing that many angles costs real money and nobody does it for an offer that loses. A high impression base and a big spread of creatives means it is worth sitting down and studying properly.

Once you find a scaling offer you have two moves, and only two.

  • Same niche, slightly different offer. Keep the buyer, change the mechanism or the outcome enough that you are not a copy. They sell a lead system to gyms, you sell the follow up system that stops those leads going cold.
  • Same offer, different niche. Keep the offer, move the buyer. An AI receptionist that is scaling for dental clinics will very likely work for physio clinics, because the problem is the same: missed calls when every hand is busy.

Doing it by hand

  1. Open the Meta Ad Library and set the country to where you want to sell.
  2. Search a niche or an offer keyword. "AI receptionist", "missed calls", "dental clinic leads", "automation for tradies". Try five or six searches, not one.
  3. Filter to active ads only. Dead ads tell you what did not work.
  4. Click into any page that shows up more than once. Count how many ads it has live. Under five, ignore it. Fifteen or more, write it down.
  5. Look at the start dates. An ad running for three months is paying for itself. An ad that started last week proves nothing yet.
  6. Read the first line of every ad on that page. The hooks tell you what pain the market is actually responding to.

Thirty minutes of this and you will know more about your market than most agencies that are already in it.

Way 3: find the truly underserved niche

This is the AI arbitrage way, the method Tim Chao talks about. It is slower to find and faster to sell, and it is where the biggest deals in this programme come from.

Instead of starting from a scaling ad, you go deep into a market looking for one big problem that a handful of businesses all share and nobody is solving properly. A problem that costs them real money every month. When you find it, you do not build a landing page and run ads. You go straight to those businesses with targeted outreach and solve that exact problem for them.

The offer lives less on a landing page here, although it could sit on one. It lives in how you sell: in your emails, your calls, your messages, your proposal. It runs across the board. Every touch says the same thing: I found the problem that is costing you, and here is the fix.

Two lessons in this module do this work with you. Find the Underserved Markets hunts for markets that are starving for help. Find the Custom Implementations digs into one of those markets and finds the specific build that fixes the costly problem. Together they hand you an offer that almost nobody else is selling.

Which door should you take? If you have never sold anything, take way 2 and get moving. If you like research and want a bigger ticket, take way 3. If you are somewhere in between, way 1 shows you what the market is already paying for and lets you stand next to it.

Those six lines are what your landing page says. They are what your emails say. They are what you say in the first two minutes of a call. Your offer is how you convey what you sell, everywhere, and it only has to be written once.

Do this now

Pick one of the three ways. Spend no more than an afternoon on it. Come back with one offer written in the six line formula, nothing more. Check: could a stranger in that niche read your six lines and know exactly whether it is for them? If yes, bring it to the strategy session. If no, the niche line is too wide.

What the Funnel Is Actually For

A website tells people who you are. A funnel takes one problem, says it out loud, and puts the right person on your calendar.

They leave with
A clear line in your head between the website and the funnel, so every ad and every outreach message points at the page that actually sells.

Before you build a page, you need to know which page you are building. Most people starting out build a website, send traffic to it, and wonder why nobody books. This lesson fixes that. It is short, it is education, and it changes what you build in the next three lessons.

A website and a funnel are two different things

A website is for brand awareness. It is the thing someone looks at when they already know your name and want to check you are real. It carries the top of funnel stuff: who you are, an overview of what you do, the clients you have worked with, their logos, a bit of story. It is useful. It makes you look credible.

It does not sell.

A funnel, which for us means a landing page, is built for one job. You take one specific problem you solve, and you speak about that pain point at its core level. Not an overview. Not everything you could do. One problem, the real cost of it, the way you fix it, and one action: book the call.

The key differences

WebsiteFunnel / landing page
Brand awareness. Proves you exist.Sells one thing. Gets a booked call.
Overview of everything you do.One specific problem you solve.
Clients you have worked with, logos, story.The pain point spoken at its core, then the fix.
Many pages, many links, many ways to leave.One page, one button, nowhere else to go.
Visitor is browsing.Visitor is deciding whether this is their problem.
Traffic arrives by name or by search.Traffic arrives from an ad or an outreach message about that exact problem.

Why the funnel sells and the website does not

Think about who lands on each one. Someone on your website is browsing. They are forming an opinion of you. Someone on your landing page came from an ad or a message that named their problem. They are asking one question: is this my problem, and can these people fix it? The page answers that question and nothing else, then gives them one thing to do.

That is why the page speaks about the pain at its core. A plumbing business does not want "AI automation". They want the phone answered when every hand is on a job, because every missed call is a job that went to the next plumber. The landing page says that. The website says "we build AI systems for trades". One of those makes someone book a call.

WHAT THE FUNNEL HOLDS

  • Who it is for, in one line, so the right person knows they are in the right place.
  • The problem and what it costs them, in their words.
  • How you fix it, the mechanism, in plain language.
  • Proof if you have it. A working demonstration if you do not.
  • One button. Book the call.

You can have both. Point everything at the funnel.

Have a website if you want one. Put the logos on it when you have them. But every ad you run in the ads module, and every message you send in the outreach module, points at the landing page. Never at the website. The website is where people go after they already want to work with you, to check you are real.

The copy on the landing page is not new work. It is your offer from Module 2, said out loud to one person with one problem. If the offer is sharp, the page is easy. If the page is hard to write, go back to the offer.

The Thank You Page

They booked. The thank you page confirms it, tells them exactly what to do next, and keeps selling while they wait.

They leave with
A thank you page with a short video, the next steps, and whatever proof you have, so the person who booked actually shows up.

Most people treat the thank you page as a receipt. "You're booked, see you then." That is a wasted page. The person on it has just made a decision. They are more open to you right now than they will be again until the call. This page uses that.

What the page does

  1. Confirms they booked a time. The date, the time in their timezone, and where the call happens. Clear, at the top, readable without watching anything.
  2. Carries a detailed video. You, to camera, telling them what to do next and building rapport. This is the centre of the page.
  3. Tells them the next steps. Accept the meeting in their calendar. Reply yes to the confirmation email or SMS. Bring the one or two things that make the call useful.
  4. Shows proof. This is the big one. Testimonials if you have them. Proof of why your system, why you, why your business. If you do not have testimonials yet, a working demonstration of the system does the job honestly.

The video

Sixty to ninety seconds. Phone, natural light, the way you talk on a call. It is not a pitch. It is you being a real person who is about to help them. Here is the shape.

  • Open. Thanks for booking, here is who I am in one line, and here is what the call is for.
  • The three asks. Accept the calendar invite so it is locked in. Reply yes to the email or text you are about to get, so I know you have seen it. Have a rough idea of how a job comes into your business and where it gets messy, because that is what we will dig into.
  • What they get. On the call we work out exactly where the problem is costing you and whether what we build fits. If it does not, I will tell you straight.
  • Rapport. One sentence about why you do this, or who you have done it for. Real, not polished.
  • Close. See you on the day.

If you have proof, say it in the video too. "We did this for a roofing company in Brisbane, you will see it on this page." If you do not, do not fake it. Show the system working instead.

Proof on the page

Under the video: testimonials, with names and businesses where you have permission. Screenshots of results that are real. A short demonstration of the system doing the job, if that is what you have. Then a few honest answers to the things that stop people showing up: what if we already use software, how much of my time does this take, what access do you need, what does support look like after. Answer each in two or three lines. Reassure with specifics, not a list of disclaimers.

Module 5 · Client Acquisition: Paid Ads · Lesson 1 of 2

How Ads Actually Work

The most consistent way to scale this business. Simple, repeatable, and almost entirely about what you say on camera.

They leave with
A clear picture of how paid ads work for this offer, your four target numbers, and the rule for scaling, fixing or killing every ad.

Ads are one of the best things you can crack in this business. Outreach gets you your first clients and teaches you the market. Ads are what scale it. This lesson is the education piece: how paid ads actually work for an offer like yours, which numbers decide whether you are winning, and the rules for what to do with every ad you run. The next lesson gives you the three skills that do the work.

Why ads are worth cracking

Ads are the most consistent way to scale overall. Not the cheapest at the start, not the easiest, but the most consistent. Outreach depends on how many calls you make this week. An ad runs while you sleep, in every city you choose, and it tells you within days whether a message works.

It is also simple and repeatable. Strip away the jargon and the whole game is this: test as many angles as possible and find as many winners as possible. An angle is one way of talking about your offer. One angle might lead with the problem that is costing the buyer money. Another leads with the result they want. Another leads with the objection they already have in their head. You put each one in front of real people, the market tells you which ones it responds to, and you make more of those.

Andromeda changed the job

Since Meta launched Andromeda, its AI ranking system, everything with ads changed. You used to open the campaign and choose who to target: ages, interests, job titles, cities. Now what you say in your video is what targets the user. Meta watches who stops, who watches, who clicks, and it finds more of those people on its own. The creative is the targeting.

So it is less about what you do in the campaign setup and more about what you say and who you say it to. If your ad opens with "if you run a plumbing business with more than five vans and your office is drowning in quotes", Meta will find plumbers with five vans and a drowning office. If your ad opens with "AI is changing everything", Meta will find people who like watching videos about AI, and almost none of them will buy a five figure install.

This is good news. The thing that decides your results is the thing you control completely: the words, and the person they are aimed at.

Being general does not work here

Being general does not really work, especially for what you are selling. If you were selling a common ecom product, a hoodie or a water bottle, you could be more general, and even then the people who win tend to find pockets.

You are selling a specific system to a specific kind of business owner with a specific pain. The more precisely your ad names that person and that pain, the cheaper every number in your account gets. This is exactly why you did the market work in the offer module before you got here. The underserved market, the problem they already know they have, the words they use for it: that research is your ad. You are not inventing anything new. You are saying the offer out loud to a stranger who has eight seconds.

The numbers: what a customer can cost you

At the end of the day, if you can acquire a customer for less than you get paid, it technically works. That is the floor. The number you are measuring is CAC, cost to acquire a customer: all the ad spend it took, divided by the number of clients who paid.

Ideally you want, at the very least, a 50 percent CAC. Meaning it costs you half of what you charge to acquire that customer. Sell for $5,000, spend $2,500 to get that client. Blake would class that as a pretty bad CAC, but it still works, because there is more than one ad and more than one month. The point is that it does not break you while you learn.

People with deep pockets who bank on upsells and recurring income can sometimes acquire a customer through ads unprofitably, on purpose, because they make it back later. That is not you yet. For you the recommendation is obvious: get it as low as possible, and never run a number you cannot afford to lose while an ad is still being tested.

It also depends on your ticket size. The cost of a qualified booked meeting for a $1,000 offer is very different to the cost of one for a $35,000 offer. At the higher price you can afford a higher cost for everything, the click, the lead, the booked call, because you are getting paid much more when one lands. There is a trade: there are fewer people who can pay that amount. The pool gets smaller as you scale up the dollar amount. Smaller pool, more expensive to reach, but each one is worth a lot more.

Work backwards from your price. If you sell at $8,000 and you want CAC under 25 percent, you have $2,000 to spend per client won. If you close one in four qualified calls, each booked call can cost up to $500. If one in three leads books, each lead can cost up to about $165. Those are your targets, before you spend a dollar. The ratios here are illustrative; yours come from your own calls.

One winner, many versions

Once you find a winning angle, meaning an ad that is performing well against the numbers above, the move is not to go hunting for a completely new idea. Create multiple versions of that same ad, over and over. Same angle, same core message, different opening line. Same script, different setting. Same words, a static image version instead of video.

Two reasons. Every ad wears out, and a fresh version resets the cost. And you learned something expensive: this angle works for this market. Every version starts from a proven base, where a brand new angle starts from zero.

A rough rhythm that works: test three to five new angles, find one winner, make five versions of it, run those while you test the next batch of angles. The library of winners grows and the account gets more stable each month.

Scale, fix or kill

Every ad you run ends up in one of three places, and only one of them is good. The skill is knowing which one you are in and acting on it quickly. Give each ad enough spend to be judged. As a working rule, about the cost of one or two booked calls per ad before you decide, and never judge an ad on its first day.

  2. Fix. One number is broken and the rest are fine. High CTR but expensive leads means the page does not match the ad. Cheap leads but no bookings means your qualifying questions or booking flow are the problem. Plenty of bookings but low show rate means the follow up is weak. Fix the one broken step. Do not rewrite the ad.
  3. Kill. It has had its fair spend and the cost per qualified call is well over target, or nobody is clicking at all. Turn it off and write down why in one line. A dead ad with a reason attached is data. A dead ad you keep running out of hope is just a bill.

Log the decision on every ad in your CRM: the ad, the outcome, the reason, the next action. In a month that record tells you exactly what this market responds to.

One Conversation, Two Calls

You are not selling on the first call. You are diagnosing. The sale happens when you come back with the fix.

They leave with
A clear picture of the two call close, what each call is for, and what has to happen in between.

Most people who try to sell AI services lose the deal on the first call, and they lose it in the first thirty seconds. They open by thanking the prospect for their time, pitch a solution before they understand the problem, and quote a number that has nothing to do with what the problem costs. The prospect nods, says they will think about it, and never replies.

This module teaches a different shape. One conversation, split across two calls. Call one is pure diagnosis. Call two is the prescription and the close. Between them, you and Jarvis do the work that makes the second call feel inevitable.

The doctor frame

Think about how a specialist runs an appointment. You booked in because something hurts. The doctor does not open with gratitude that you turned up, and they do not quote a surgery fee before they have examined you. They ask what is wrong, how long it has been going on, what you have tried, how it affects your life. Then they go away, look at the results, and come back with a plan.

That is the frame. They booked into your calendar because something in your marketing hit a pain. They have come to you for the solution. You are the prize in that room, and you act like it: calm, curious, in control of the sequence. Not arrogant. A good doctor is warm. But a good doctor is never grateful you showed up.

This matters more than any script. If you believe you are lucky to be on the call, every question you ask will sound like permission seeking, and every number you say will sound like an apology.

What call one is for

Call one has exactly one job: understand their business well enough to put a dollar figure on what is broken, and get them to agree to that figure out loud. That is it. You do not pitch. You do not demo. You do not say a price.

The reason is simple. You cannot price a problem you have not measured, and a price with no measured problem behind it is just a number they compare to other numbers. When you come back on call two with a figure that is a fraction of what the problem costs them, the comparison is already won.

Why no price on call one

Three reasons, and the third is the one most people miss.

  1. You do not know the number yet. Your price is a percentage of what the problem costs. Until they have told you what it costs, any price is a guess.
  2. You have not earned it yet. A price lands differently after someone has watched you understand their business better than their last three suppliers did.
  3. You need the gap to get a real build cost. If you are using a technical partner (Module 7 covers how), you take the brief from call one to them, ask what they will charge to build it fully, and mark up from there. If you are building it with Jarvis yourself, you still want a day to scope it properly. Quoting on call one means quoting blind.

When they ask on call one, and some will, you have an honest answer.

I could throw a number at you now, but it would be a guess, and you deserve better than a guess. Let me go away, map exactly what this would look like for you, get the real cost of building it, and come back with a proper proposal. If the numbers make sense we move. If they don't, you've lost forty minutes and gained a free audit.

Below is the full structure, with the words to say at each step. Read it, then print the card at the end and keep it next to your screen. You will stop needing it after ten calls. Use it for the first ten anyway.

You are the prize

They booked into your calendar. Something in your marketing hit a pain point and they want the solution. So do not jump on the call and open with "thank you for jumping on today". That line puts them as the chooser and you as the applicant. It sounds polite. It costs you the frame before you have asked a single question.

Act as a doctor would. You are there to diagnose their business, understand the problems, then prescribe a solution. Warm, curious, unhurried. Never grateful.

The open

Thirty seconds of human, then straight into the question. No agenda slide. No company story.

Hey [name], good to meet you properly. How's the week going?

[Let them answer. One line back. Then:]

This one question does two things. It gets them talking about pain in their own words, and it tells you which of two branches you are in. Listen for it.

Branch A: problem aware

They name a specific problem straight away. This is the usual case when your ad or outreach called out a particular pain: most people come wanting a fix to that thing. Your job is to get them explaining it in their own words, then understand how it specifically and uniquely affects their business. Efficiency, effectiveness, or money. How long has it been like that?

Do not fix anything yet. Do not say "we can automate that". Every time you jump to a solution you stop them talking, and what they say next is the material you will sell them with.

What do they have now?

The four questions that find the number

This is the core of the call. Everything before it was warming up. These four questions, in this order, turn a vague complaint into a dollar figure they will agree to.

  1. What is actually happening in the business? The problem as a sequence of events, not an adjective.
  2. What are they doing to try and solve it currently? Tools, people, workarounds, nothing.
  3. Who currently does all this work? Names, roles, hours. This is where wages enter the picture.
  4. How much is it costing you right now? The cost of not fixing it. Employee cost. Subscriptions. Lost jobs. Whatever the cost is, find its dollar value.

Get the yes. If they push back on the number, lower it until they agree. You want a figure they own, not a figure you sold them. A conservative number they said yes to beats an impressive number they rolled their eyes at.

Once you understand the pain, it is about quantifying the cost of it, whether that is time, money, or efficiency and effectiveness. Money is always the driving factor. If it is efficiency, it is about making their money better spent. If it is staff, they want to remove the manual work and reduce overhead, and overhead is cash. If it is missed calls, each one was a job. Your whole goal in discovery is to find out how much this problem costs them.

If you work out the problem costs them $150k a year, charging 5 to 10 percent of that is a no brainer. How that number gets used is the next two lessons.

Prescribe the Solution

They gave you a problem and a number. Now you design the thing that fixes it, check it can be built, and price it against the problem.

This is the lesson most people in this space never learn, because they came from building and not from selling. They price the work. Hours, tokens, licences, a margin on top. And then they wonder why a $4,000 quote gets ghosted and a $22,000 one gets signed in the same week.

You are going to price the problem. This lesson shows you how, using a real deal.

Price the problem, not the work

The key concept is understanding their pain specifically and charging accordingly. You need to know how much the current problem is costing them, which is exactly what call one produced.

If a problem is costing them $2k a year and you are trying to charge $20k to solve it, that is a huge disconnect, and no amount of charm fixes it. Your pricing is based on their problem, within reason. Technically you could charge $50k for a new website if the problem is costing them $5 million a year, assuming the solution is worthy. To them it is worth it. If that $50k genuinely solves the problem, financially it makes sense. It is an investment in solving the problem, and at that point you are not pitching, you are solving.

The Australian government spent $96 million on a website. Yes, government contracts are bloated. Yes, it is an extreme example. But because they had the budget and the problem cost them a certain amount, they could foresee spending $4.6 million on a website, let alone $96 million. Price follows problem size. It always has.

The working range

Charge 5 to 10 percent of the problem's annual cost for the build, and show the running costs separately and honestly. Anchor at 10, land at 5. The exact mechanics of saying it out loud are in Call Two. For now, the maths.

The $22,000 AI CRM

A high ticket construction company. Their pain was four things, found on call one.

2. The cost. $13k a year in platform costs alone. Their previous CRM also charged per job to send invoices and receive payments, which meant $100 per new customer account.

3. Manual tasks done by one employee which could 100 percent be removed. She only worked three days a week but cost them over $65k a year.

4. Job efficiency, start to completion. Each job took several weeks. By introducing a few automations into the CRM, they could add an extra four jobs a week with no extra staff.

Their problem was costing them around $110k a year, plus the extra jobs they could not handle with their current stack, probably another $500k a year. So when the price was $22k, it was an easy sell.

Look at the structure of that, because it is the structure of every good deal. Four distinct pains, each with a number. A cost side (platform, per account fees, wages) and a revenue side (jobs they could not take). A price that is a small fraction of the cost side alone, before you even mention the upside. The client is not deciding whether to spend $22k. They are deciding whether to keep spending $110k.

What a prescribed solution looks like

A prescription is not a feature list. It is three things, in order.

  1. What gets installed. The system, named plainly. "A custom CRM with automated invoicing and job scheduling", not "an AI powered operations layer".
  2. In what order. What goes live first, what follows, what waits. First thing live should hit the biggest pain, so they feel the win early.
  3. What each piece is worth. Map each part of the build back to one of the pains and its number. If a piece does not map to a pain, cut it. It is cost with no story.

Then the proposal (next lesson) wraps that in their words and a demo.

Get the real build cost before you price

For a two call close you do not need to talk about price on call one, and that gap is exactly what lets you take the brief to the person who will complete the work and ask what they will charge to fully build the project. You mark up from there. If you are self fulfilling with Jarvis (Module 7), you scope it yourself the same way: what has to be built, what it connects to, how long, what it costs to run.

Two inputs set your price. The problem cost sets what they will pay. The build cost sets whether you can deliver it profitably. If 5 percent of the problem does not clear your build cost with margin, either the problem is too small or the solution is too big. Fix one before call two.

The proposal must show the running costs

When you sell the AI and create the proposal, it has to show the actual ongoing costs of the system: what the AI, the subscriptions and whatever else they need to run it will actually cost each month. Very important. Hiding it feels clever until month two, when their card gets charged and they call you. Put it on the page, next to the build price, and it becomes part of the value story: "you were paying $13k a year to a platform that would not change. Illustrative versus connected demos

There are two honest kinds of demo and one dishonest kind. Know which you are showing and say it on the page.

  • Illustrative. A mock up or a clickable walk through with made up data, showing the shape of the workflow. Fast to build with Jarvis. Completely fine for call two as long as it is labelled, quietly, as a mock up.
  • Connected. A working slice that actually talks to a real tool, using sandbox or test records. Slower, more convincing, worth it for bigger deals or when they have been burned by vapourware before.
  • The dishonest kind. An animation that implies the integration is built and live when it is not. Do not. It wins the call and loses the client at onboarding.

Keep the demo to three to five visible steps and about a minute of talking. Show the screen or output they will actually use, not a dashboard full of fictional revenue. Call Two: Present and Close

Everything on this call was set up by the last one. Present the proposal properly, speak like the doctor, say the number, and then stop talking.

They leave with

Call two is where the money is, and it is the easier of the two calls, provided call one did its job. You are not persuading anyone. You are presenting a proposal that fixes a problem they already told you costs them real money, at a fraction of that money. This lesson is two things: how to present the proposal properly, and how to speak while you do it. Get both right and a no means choosing to keep the problem.

Between the calls, you and Jarvis draft the proposal from your call one notes, and you bring the recording and the draft to the weekly session so Blake or the technical CSM can check the diagnosis and the scope. Do not present a proposal nobody has checked.

How to present the proposal

Four moves, in order. Recap their words. Walk the plan tied to their problem. Show the demo. Then the number. Never skip the recap, and never say a price before the demo.

1. RECAP THEIR WORDS AND COLLECT THE YESES

Play their own words back to them. Their problem, their number. Every sentence ends in a question they can only answer yes to. Each yes puts them in a more agreeable state for the rest of the call.

[Name], good to see you again. Before I show you anything, I want to make sure I've got this right, because if I've misunderstood something the rest of this is pointless.

Last time you told me [the problem as events, their phrase]. Is that still the picture?

And right now that's being handled by [current fix], with [person] spending about [hours] a week on it. Right?

And when we added it up, between [wages], [platform] and [lost jobs], we landed on this costing you around [number] a year. Is that still a fair number?

Okay. So this current system is costing you [number] a year. Good. Let me show you what I'd do about it.

If they revise the number down, let them and use theirs. A number they own beats a number they tolerate.

2. WALK THE PLAN, TIED TO THEIR PROBLEM

Walk sections one to three of the proposal. Problem, number, what changes. Slowly. Pause after each and ask if it matches how they see it. Every feature you mention gets attached to a sentence they said on call one. If you cannot attach it, leave it out.

So here's what I found. The thing underneath [the problem] is actually [root cause in plain words]. That's why [the current fix] keeps not working: it's built for [what it was built for], not for how you actually run jobs.

What I'd put in is [the system, named plainly]. Here's how a [job, lead, order] moves through it now, and here's how it moves through it after. The difference is [the two or three things that change]. [Person] stops doing [the manual work] and starts doing [the valuable thing].

Does that match how you'd want it to work? Anything in there that wouldn't fit?

3. SHOW THE DEMO

Share your screen and step through it. Three to five steps, about a minute. Narrate what the business is doing, not what the software is doing. Make a point of the exception step, because that is where they see their own person using it.

This is one job going through it. Here's the request coming in. Here's the system doing [the first thing] on its own. Now here's one where it's not sure, see, the [field] is missing, so it flags it and [person] takes ten seconds to fix it instead of an hour to chase it. And here's where it lands in [their system].

This is a mock up, built on your workflow, so you can see the shape. The real one plugs into [their tools].

Can you see [person] using that?

4. ANCHOR, THEN SAY THE NUMBER

You already have a yes on the problem cost from the recap. Now you ladder down from it, collecting a yes at each rung, and the last rung is your price. Then you go silent.

So [name], let's say I came to you with a solution to your [problem cost] a year problem, and let's say that solution only cost 10 percent of the total problem's cost. As a business owner, does that sound like a viable investment? You pay 10 percent to solve 100 percent of the problem and keep over [90 percent of the number] a year?

[Wait for the yes. If they hedge: it's only a thought exercise, work with me here.]

Well, I agree, 10 percent would be a fair charge to solve this. But I'm not charging you 10 percent. I'm not even charging you 7.5 percent. I'm charging you 5 percent of the total problem's cost to solve it for you, so [price], and it'll be done in [x] days.

[Stop. Do not speak. Let the silence sit until they fill it.]

If they go quiet at the 10 percent question, say it is a thought exercise and ask again. The yes still comes. The same move in dollars, the version Blake used on a $150k problem:

So this is costing you about $150k a year. If I could remove that for $50k, would that be a smart business investment? Take $150k of expense off the books for $50k?

[Yeah, I guess.]

Good. I'm not going to charge you $50k. It's $5k to set the whole thing up, and $500 a month for maintenance and any changes after that.

[Silence.]

Anchored at fifty, five is a no brainer. The number they hear last is always a fraction of a number they already agreed to.

How to speak

The structure above fails in the mouth of someone who rushes it. Three habits.

  • Slow down. Slower than feels natural. Fast sounds like getting it over with before they object. Slow sounds like someone who has done this before.
  • Silence after the number. Say the price, then stop. It will feel like an hour. It is usually five seconds. Whoever speaks first after the price is the one who moves, and it is not you.
  • Their words, not yours. If they called it "the office being flat out", you call it that too. The moment you translate their pain into your language, it becomes your idea instead of their problem.

The one asking the questions is in control

The person asking the questions is the person running the call. When you are presenting, it feels like you should be talking. You should not. Every move above is built around a question because the question hands them the next step and makes them say it. Three minutes into a monologue, stop and ask something. If they start interrogating you, answer in two sentences and ask a question back. You are the doctor. Doctors ask.

The same rule covers reframing. When a prospect frames the price as a cost, you do not argue. You ask a question that puts it back next to the problem: "Compared to the [number] a year it's costing you now, where does [price] sit?"

Close and set next steps

When they speak again, it is a yes, a question, or an objection. A yes goes straight to logistics. A question gets answered, then you ask again. An objection goes to the next lesson. Either way you are not chasing. You are scheduling the treatment.

[On a yes:] Great. Here's how it works from here. Objections

An objection is not a no. It is the last piece of pain you have not found yet. Identify it, then prescribe.

They leave with

Nobody argues a prospect into a deal. If you find yourself arguing on call two, you have left the doctor frame and become a salesperson, and they can feel it. Every objection below is handled the same way: stay calm, find the real concern underneath it, and prescribe. You already know how to do that. It is call one, in miniature.

Before the list, the one line that handles most of them. When someone hesitates without saying why, do not guess. Sort it.

What's the part you're sitting on? Is it the money, the timing, or whether this actually works in your business?

Three buckets. Every objection below lives in one of them.

1. "It's too expensive"

The frame. Expensive compared to what? If the anchor landed, the price is 5 percent of a number they agreed to. If they still say expensive, either the number was not really theirs, or the problem is not actually a priority. Go back to the number, not the price.

Fair enough. Let me check something, because I'd rather you say no than buy something that doesn't make sense. We agreed this is costing you around [number] a year, right? So the question isn't really whether [price] is a lot. It's whether you'd rather keep paying [number] a year. If the number's wrong, tell me, and we'll re-look at the whole thing.

2. "I need to think about it"

The frame. Nobody thinks about it. They either have an unspoken objection or no urgency. Respectfully refuse the vague version and sort it.

Of course. Can I ask, what specifically do you want to think through? Because if it's something I can answer now, I'd rather do that than have you sit with a question for a week. Is it the money, the timing, or whether it'll work for you?

The frame. If you asked on call one who else needed to be in the room, this rarely happens. If it does, do not let the partner hear a second hand version of the pitch. Get them on a call.

Makes sense, it's their business too. The thing is, if you explain it to them you'll get the half of it you remember, and they'll ask questions you can't answer, and it'll die in the kitchen. Let's do fifteen minutes with both of you, I'll run the short version. When are they free?

4. "We tried AI and it didn't work"

The frame. This is gold. Ask what they tried. It will almost always be a generic tool bolted on with no workflow design, which is exactly what you are not selling. Their bad experience becomes your proof.

What did you try? [Listen.] Yeah, that's the pattern. Someone plugged a chatbot in and hoped. The frame. A proposal read alone, without you, is a price list. Never send it as a substitute for call two. Send it as a follow up to call two.

The frame. Timing objections are cost of delay objections. You have the annual number. Divide it.

Totally get it. Just so we're both looking at the same thing: at [number] a year, next quarter costs you about [number divided by four] in the meantime, and that's before the jobs you can't take. The frame. This is a fulfilment question wearing an objection costume. Answer it with the support line in the proposal and the exception step they saw in the demo.

Good question, and it's why the support line is on page five. For [period] after launch I'm the one who fixes it, included. The frame. Do not invent a guarantee you have not agreed with yourself. Guarantee the thing you control: the build, the launch, the support. Not their revenue.

I can't guarantee your revenue, and anyone who says they can is lying to you. What I guarantee is this: it'll do the things on page five, it'll be live by [date], and if it doesn't do what we agreed, I keep working on it at no extra fee until it does. That's a promise about my work, which is the only honest promise I can make.

The frame. Maybe they could. Agree, then ask about the cost of the attempt. You are selling the outcome by a date, not the code.

You probably could, honestly, the tools are out there. The question is whether you want [person] learning to build software for the next three months instead of doing their job, and what it costs you if version one doesn't quite work. You're not paying me for the code. You're paying to have it running by [date], done by someone who's done it before, so you can get back to [their actual business].

The frame. Sometimes true. If the problem cost is genuinely small, say so and walk away with grace; that honesty brings referrals. If the number is real, it is not about size.

Maybe. Let's look at it. The problem's costing you [number] a year. That's not a big company problem or a small company problem, it's just [number] leaving the business. If it were [much smaller number] I'd tell you not to bother and I'd mean it. At [number], it's worth fixing.

Two Ways To Deliver

You have money in the bank and a promise to keep. The first decision is who builds it.

They leave with
A decision rule you can apply to any signed deal: arbitrage it or build it yourself.

Call two is done. The deposit has landed. Now you owe a business owner a working system, and the way you deliver it decides whether this deal makes you money or eats the next three weeks of your life. There are two ways to deliver, and this lesson gives you a rule for choosing between them before you touch a single build.

The two paths

Arbitrage. You sold it. A vetted developer builds it. You scope the job properly, hand them a build plan, pay them a fixed price in milestones, and keep the difference between what the client paid and what the developer cost. Your job is the client, the scope and the money. Their job is the build.

Self fulfilment. You sold it and you build it, with Jarvis doing most of the actual work. Same scoping plan, but instead of handing it to a person, you hand it to Jarvis one piece at a time, test it, and deliver it yourself. Higher margin, more of your hours, and it only works if you are comfortable in the tools.

The honest recommendation

If you are not technical, bring someone technical in to complete the job. Every time. Even if you want to do the creative side yourself, the workflow design, the client conversations, the demo, the way it feels to use. Do that part. Then hand the build to someone who already knows how to wire it, rather than trying to understand the whole thing from scratch while a paying client waits.

This is not a comment on your ability. It is a comment on where your hours are worth the most. You just proved you can find a business owner, diagnose a real problem and get paid five figures to fix it. That skill is rare and it is the one that scales. Learning to debug an integration at eleven at night is not rare, and plenty of good people will do it for a fixed fee. The members who stall in this programme are almost never the ones who could not sell. They are the ones who sold, then disappeared into a build they were not ready for.

The programme keeps a vetted developer list and the weekly group strategy session exists for exactly this. If you have signed a deal and you are not sure who should build it, bring it to the session before you start.

  2. It is built on test data. The client's live records are never the place to find out the system has a bug.
  3. It is tested like the client will use it before they ever see it.
  4. Handover is a process, not a link in an email. Onboarding, training, sign off, invoice, support window.

Arbitrage: Scope, Hand Off, Keep the Margin

A bad handover is how a profitable deal turns into three weeks of unpaid project management.

They leave with
A full build plan from the scoping agent, handed to a developer with milestones, a budget and a date, all logged against the deal.

By the time a deal is signed you already know more about this client's business than anyone who is about to build for them. You ran call one. You heard the pain in their words. Between the calls you and the technical lead designed the fix and priced it. Call two walked them through it and they said yes. All of that lives in notes, a proposal and your head. This lesson turns it into one document a developer can build from without a single clarifying call, and that document is what protects your margin.

Why the scope is the whole job

Every expensive build problem is a scoping problem wearing a disguise. The developer "misunderstood" because the brief said automate intake and never said what happens when the email has no phone number. The client "changed their mind" because the proposal said quoting system and they pictured something that also chased payments. The project "ran long" because nobody wrote down which of their three spreadsheets was the source of truth. All of it was knowable on the day the deal closed. Writing it down then costs an hour. Finding it out later costs the margin.

The scoping agent

The scoping agent is a skill in the High Ticket Skill Pack you loaded in Module 1. If you skipped that step, paste the skill file into Jarvis now and ask it to install it as a skill called jarvis-scoping-agent. Its job is simple to describe and tedious to do by hand: take everything you know about this job and write the full build plan, per job, in a fixed shape a builder can work from.

You feed it four things, and the more raw the better:

  • Your call one notes or transcript, the brief you built from them in Module 6, and the number you agreed.
  • Your call two notes, including anything they asked for, pushed back on, or added at the end.
  • The proposal exactly as presented, with the prescribed solution and the price.
  • Anything the client sent: screenshots of their current system, a spreadsheet export, a list of the tools they use, the people who will touch it.

Most of them are one message to the client: which spreadsheet is current, who approves a quote over a certain size, what their booking tool is called. Send that message today. Then read the edge cases. If the client never mentioned what happens when a job comes in with no address, decide now and write it in, because the developer will otherwise decide it for you.

Do not send the plan to a developer with unknowns still open. A plan with holes gets a quote with holes, and the holes become change requests at your cost.

The handoff

  1. Send the plan, ask for a fixed quote against the three milestones. Give them two working days. Tell them to list anything in the plan they would do differently and why.
  2. Agree the price and the dates in writing, on the platform if you are using one. Fixed price per milestone, released on acceptance. Never a hundred percent up front. A deposit for milestone one is normal.
  3. Give them test data and sandbox access, never the client's live accounts. If the client's tool has no sandbox, export a sample with real names removed.
  4. Set the rhythm. A short written update at the end of every working day, a screen recording at every milestone, and questions go to you, not the client.
  5. Log it against the deal. Developer, quote, milestone dates, what has been paid. Jarvis keeps this in your CRM so nothing lives only in a chat thread.

You are the account manager, not the messenger. When the developer asks a question you do not know the answer to, ask the client as if it is your question, because it is. The client bought you.

Keeping the margin

The margin is lost in three places: scope that grew without a price change, milestones paid before they were checked, and your own hours disappearing into chasing. The plan fixes the first. Acceptance tests fix the second: a milestone is paid when the tests pass on a recording you watched, not when the developer says it is done. The daily update fixes the third, because you read one message a day instead of asking.

If the client asks for something that is not in the plan, the answer is warm and fixed: "Good idea. It is not in this build, so I will scope it as a follow on once we are live." Then write it in the retainer conversation in the last lesson of this module. Scope creep is not a problem, it is the next invoice, as long as it never gets built for free.

Illustrative, not a client result. A member sells a quoting system for a landscaping business. The proposal says quotes are generated from a site visit form. Call two notes show the owner said, almost as an aside, that half the enquiries come in by text message. The scoping agent catches it and puts "text message intake: in scope or follow on?" at the top of the unknowns. The member asks, the owner says it matters, and the member scopes it as a $1,800 follow on rather than discovering it as an unpaid surprise in week three.

Self Fulfilment: Build It With Jarvis

The same operator that sold it can build most of it. This is where the free course skills pay off at ten times the price.

They leave with
The first working version of the client's system, built and running on test data.

If you chose self fulfilment, you are about to find out that the build is not one task. It is twenty small ones, done in order, each checked before the next. Jarvis does most of the typing. You do the ordering, the checking and the judgement. The members who struggle here are not short on skill, they asked Jarvis to build the whole system in one message and then spent a week untangling what came back.

Same plan, different builder

You still start with the scoping agent. Read the previous lesson if you skipped it, because nothing changes about the plan when you are the builder. Problem summary, modules, integrations, data in and out, edge cases, human steps, milestones, acceptance tests, unknowns. Clear the unknowns with the client first. The only sections you do not need are the budget split and the developer dates, and even then, give yourself dates. A build with no go live date drifts.

The plan matters more when you build it yourself, not less, because there is no second person to notice when you wander. Every time you are tempted to add something, the question is: is it in the plan? If not, it waits.

Build the way Jarvis builds best

One module at a time. One change per message. Test data only. This is the same discipline you learned building your first service in the free course, and it is why those skills pay off here at ten times the price.

  1. Set up the shell first. The empty app, the empty tables, the connections to the tools with test credentials. Nothing clever. Confirm each connection reads and writes one record before moving on.
  2. Build modules in the order the plan lists them, which should be the order a job moves through the system. Intake before processing, processing before output. Do not build the dashboard first. Nobody has ever been paid for a dashboard that shows nothing.
  3. For each module, paste the module's section of the plan and ask for only that. When it is built, run its acceptance test from the plan. If it passes, move on. If not, fix that one thing before touching anything else.
  4. Edge cases get their own messages. Build the happy path for a module, test it, then add each edge case from the plan as its own change: missing field, duplicate, uncertain match. Each one tested on its own.
  5. Human steps get built as real steps, a status a person has to change, a message a person has to receive. Do not let Jarvis quietly automate a decision the plan says a person makes.

Going back and forth

The build is a conversation. Jarvis builds a piece, you look at it with a real example in your hand, you tell it what is wrong in plain words, it fixes it. The skill is in how you describe what is wrong. "It doesn't work" produces guesses. "I sent the sample email with no phone number and it created a job with a blank phone field instead of flagging it for review like the plan says" produces a fix.

Three habits that keep this fast:

  • Describe the input, what happened, and what should have happened. Every time, in that order.
  • One fix per message. If three things are wrong, send three messages. Batching them is how the second fix undoes the first.
  • Ask it to explain before it changes anything big. "Before you change this, tell me in two sentences what you think is causing it." Half the time you will spot the real cause from the explanation.

Keep the client's live data out of the build. Every record you test on is made up or a sample with real names removed. The first time real data touches the system is the controlled go live in the handover lesson, with the client watching.

When to stop and get help

Some things are not worth a day of your time. If a single integration has eaten more than a couple of hours, if the tool genuinely cannot do something the plan assumes, or if you have fixed the same bug three times, stop. Bring it to the weekly strategy session, or hand that one module to a developer from your bench at a fixed price while you keep building the rest. Arbitrage and self fulfilment are not a religion. Most experienced members do both inside the same deal.

Illustrative, not a client result. A member builds an enquiry triage system for a plumbing business. Intake, classification and the job record take two evenings with Jarvis and pass their tests. Connecting to the client's booking tool turns out to need an API the member has never used. Instead of a lost weekend, they pay a developer from the bench a fixed $400 to wire that one connection against the plan's integration section, test it with the plan's acceptance test, and keep building the follow up module while they wait.

Self Fulfilment: Test It Like the Client Will

Clients do not use systems the way you built them. Find that out yourself, in private.

They leave with
A tested build with the edge cases handled and a written test log you can show them.

Every module passed its own test. That is not the same as the system working. The client is going to put a real job through it, start to finish, on a busy Tuesday, from their phone, with a typo in the email address. This lesson is short because the idea is simple: before the client sees it, you run it the way they will, end to end, and you fix what breaks in private.

Why this is not optional

The first time a client sees their system is the moment they decide whether you are a professional. If the first job they push through it gets stuck, the build could be ninety five percent perfect and they will remember the five. If it runs clean, they will forgive small things for months. Testing is not about finding bugs. It is about controlling the first impression of something you have been paid five figures for.

It is also where the retainer gets sold without saying a word. A client who watches a system handle a messy edge case properly understands why it is worth paying to keep it running.

End to end, not module by module

Module tests check parts. This test checks the whole path. Take three realistic jobs and push each one through from the very first touch to the very last output.

  1. The clean job. Everything present, everything normal. It should go all the way through with no human stepping in except where the plan says they do.
  2. The messy job. A missing field, a wrong format, a duplicate of a job already in the system. Each should land where the plan says, flagged for a person, not silently dropped and not silently guessed.
  3. The job from the client's seat. Do it on a phone, logged in as the role that will actually use it, not as you with full access. Half the problems you find will be about what that person can and cannot see.

Go back and forth with Jarvis exactly as you did in the build. Describe the input, what happened, what should have happened. One fix per message. Rerun the job after every fix, from the start, because a fix in step four can break step two.

The client experience is the product as far as they are concerned. This is also where referrals get made or lost.

The system works. Now it has to work for them, in their business, run by their people, with their real data. Done badly it becomes a month of "quick questions" and a client who never quite trusts the thing they paid for.

Before the handover call

Three things are ready before you book the call, and none of them are negotiable.

  • The test log is clean. Three end to end passes, dated. If you arbitraged the build, you ran them yourself, not the developer.
  • The client has done their part. Access to the live tools, the real records that will be loaded, the names of the people who will use it. The unknowns list from the scoping plan is empty.
  • You know who is running the call. If you are not technical, the person who built it or someone technical from your bench is on the call with you, doing the go live steps while you run the conversation. Say that plainly to yourself: you do not want to be learning the system's internals in front of the person who paid for it. You own the relationship. They own the switches.

The weekly strategy session and the programme's developer list are there for this moment too. A member on their first handover should not be doing it alone.

The handover call

Forty five minutes to an hour, on a screen share, with the owner and whoever will actually use the system day to day. It runs in five parts.

  1. Remind them what they bought and why. Two minutes. The problem in their words from call one, the number it was costing them, what this system does about it. They have been busy. Bring them back to the reason.
  2. Walk the clean job through, live. The same job from your test, on the live system now. Narrate what is happening at each step and who gets told. Then do the messy job, so they see an edge case flagged to a person rather than disappearing. This is the single most trust building thing you will do.
  3. Controlled go live. Load the first real records, with them watching. Pick one real job that is actually in their business right now and push it through. Fix nothing on the fly. If something is wrong, note it and tell them when it will be fixed.
  4. Train the people who will use it. Each role does their part once, themselves, while you watch. Not you doing it while they watch. A person who has clicked the button will click it again. A person who watched you click it will message you on Monday.
  5. Sign off and the support window. Go through the acceptance tests from the scoping plan on screen, tick them together, and ask the owner to confirm in writing that the build is delivered as scoped. Then tell them what the support window is, how to reach you in it, and what happens after it.

Support, Retainer and Raising the Price

The install was the start. Everything after it is margin, proof and a higher price next time.

They leave with
A retainer offered on your current deal, a case study written, and your next price decided.

You have been paid for the build. Three things happen in the next thirty days that decide whether that was one deal or the start of a business: you support it properly, you turn it into a monthly retainer, and you turn the result into the reason the next client pays more. None of them is hard. All of them get skipped by people who are already chasing the next sale.

The thirty day support window

Support is included for thirty days from sign off and it is defined, not vague. Included: anything that does not behave the way it was shown at handover, questions from the people using it, and small adjustments to wording, notifications or who gets told what. Not included: new modules, new integrations, or anything that was in the follow on list. Those are change requests with a price, and you say so warmly the moment one comes up.

Respond inside a working day. Fix inside two for anything that stops a job moving. Keep every request in the deal record in your CRM, because the list of what they asked for in the first month is the best possible evidence for what the retainer should cover.

What the retainer is

Systems do not run themselves. Tools update, a login expires, the client hires someone new who needs training, a volume spike shows an edge case nobody saw. The retainer is the monthly fee that keeps the system working and keeps you as the person they call. It is usually the most profitable money in the whole deal, because the work is light and it compounds across clients.

How to offer it on the day thirty call

You booked this call at handover, so it is expected. It is ten minutes. You open with the month's numbers, then you offer the retainer as the way to keep what they have. Nobody is being sold anything they have not already experienced.

Thirty days in. [Number] jobs have gone through the system, [number] were flagged for your team and cleared, and you have not had to chase a quote by hand since we went live. Two things I want to put to you. First, the system needs someone keeping it running, the tools it sits on change, your team changes, and it costs [amount] a month in platforms to run. Second, you have already asked for [the follow on items] which I noted and did not build for free. The retainer is [amount] a month. It covers the running costs, me watching it weekly, small changes as you need them, support for your team, and a one page report every month. The follow on work gets quoted separately when you want it. Want me to send that over so it starts from the first?

If they say not now, the running costs still have to be paid by someone. Transfer the accounts to them cleanly, tell them the door is open, and put a reminder in Jarvis for sixty days. A good number come back the first time something breaks.

Write the case study now

The test log, the build log and the first monthly report are a case study that has not been written yet. Write it in the first month, while the numbers are fresh and the client is pleased, and always with their written permission before anything goes on a public page, an ad or a call.

Write a one page case study from the [client] deal record: the problem in the client's words from the call one brief, what it was costing them, what we built in plain language, the before and after of how a job moves, and the results from the first monthly report with the actual numbers from the system. Separate time saved from money saved and keep revenue claims out unless the client gave us a figure. Mark any number that is an estimate. Write it so a similar business owner would recognise themselves in the first paragraph. Then write a two sentence version I can say on a call. Do not publish anything; I will get the client's written permission first.

Raise the price

Your next price is set by your last result, not by your confidence. Before this deal you were selling a promise. After it you are selling a thing that exists, with a number next to it, and that is worth more. The Module 6 pricing method does not change, the build is still priced against the problem's cost, but your range inside it moves up, and the proposal now opens with proof instead of a hypothesis.

  • Put the case study's two sentence version into call one, right after you have found the pain: "we did this for a [similar business] last month."
  • Add the retainer to every proposal from now on as a standard line, not an afterthought.
  • Decide the next price today, write it in your offer document, and tell the group session what it is so someone holds you to it.

Illustrative, not a client result. A member sells their first quoting system for $9,000 with no retainer. On day thirty they offer a $600 a month retainer and the client takes it. The case study shows quotes going out the same day instead of in three. The next proposal for a similar business opens with that result, prices the build at $14,000 against a bigger problem cost, and includes the retainer from the start. Same work. One proof point.
